# Evidence overview: AI and machine learning for protein function prediction

## Overall assessment

The supplied records contain a small number of directly relevant studies, several reviews and methodological papers that provide context, and many records that concern clinical prediction or drug discovery rather than protein-function prediction. The strongest direct evidence is the study on **structure-based protein function prediction using graph convolutional networks**. The reviews on protein knowledge, metalloproteins, protein structure prediction, and protein–protein docking are relevant to the broader methodological landscape, but the supplied bibliographic information does not reveal their specific datasets, benchmarks, or quantitative results.

A major limitation of the supplied evidence is that titles and metadata alone are insufficient to determine:

- which functional annotation databases were used;
- how sequence or structural redundancy was controlled;
- whether evaluations used random, homology-aware, or temporal splits;
- which metrics were reported;
- how well methods transferred to remote-homology or experimentally uncharacterized proteins.

The records marked below therefore require full-text verification before specific claims can be made.

## 1. Most directly relevant evidence

### 1.1 Structure-based protein function prediction with graph convolutional networks

**Gligorijević V, Renfrew PD, Kościółek T, et al. “Structure-based protein function prediction using graph convolutional networks.” 2021.**  
DOI: `10.1038/s41467-021-23303-9`

**Relevance: Very high**

This is the clearest direct match to the question. The method uses **graph convolutional networks (GCNs)** to represent protein structures, presumably as residue or atom graphs, and predict functional annotations from structural context. It is important because it represents a shift from sequence-only prediction toward models that can exploit:

- spatial proximity between residues;
- geometric and structural features;
- local structural motifs;
- relationships between residues that may be distant in sequence but close in three-dimensional space.

**What it can contribute to the review**

- A concrete example of structure-based deep learning for protein function.
- Evidence about graph representations and structural feature engineering.
- Potential comparison between sequence-based and structure-based predictions.
- Evaluation of whether predicted or experimentally determined structures can support annotation.

**Datasets and metrics:** Not ascertainable from the supplied record. Full-text verification is essential to identify the functional ontology or benchmark dataset, the source of protein structures, the train/test split strategy, and reported measures such as precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, or family-level metrics.

**Full-text verification: Required.** A local PDF is listed, but the bibliographic record alone does not establish the findings.

### 3.2 Leakage and reproducibility in machine learning

**Kapoor S, Narayanan A. “Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science.” 2023.**  
DOI: `10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804`

**Relevance: High for limitations and critical appraisal**

This paper is especially relevant to evaluating protein-function prediction benchmarks. Protein datasets are highly redundant: homologous or nearly identical sequences can occur across training and test sets. If related proteins are split randomly, performance can be inflated because the model is evaluated on examples very similar to those seen during training.

Issues to examine in the protein literature include:

- sequence homology leakage across splits;
- duplicated structures or annotations;
- annotations derived from the same evidence used to construct features;
- tuning on the test set;
- use of predicted structures generated by related models;
- incomplete or noisy negative labels;
- inconsistent annotation depth across protein families.

**Full-text verification: Required.**

# Synthesis by methodological approach

## 1. Sequence-based models and protein language models

Protein language models learn statistical representations from large collections of protein sequences. Their embeddings can be used with classifiers or regression models to predict properties such as activity, localization, family membership, interaction propensity, or functional annotations.

The supplied evidence supports this area mainly through:

- Bordin et al. (2023), a broad review of machine learning for protein knowledge;
- Tran et al. (2026), language-model-guided directed evolution;
- Zhang et al. (2025), transformer-based sequence screening.

**Strengths**

- Can operate when no experimental structure is available.
- Capture evolutionary and contextual information without manually designed features.
- Can transfer representations across related prediction tasks.
- Scale to very large sequence databases.

**Limitations**

- Predictions may reflect homology rather than mechanistic understanding.
- Performance can be inflated by sequence similarity between training and test proteins.
- Rare functions and poorly represented protein families remain difficult.
- Functional labels are often incomplete, unevenly distributed, or derived from computational annotation.
- Long proteins, multimers, disordered regions, and context-dependent functions may be difficult to model.

The exact datasets and metrics remain unknown from the supplied records.

---

## 2. Structure-based graph and geometric models

The Gligorijević et al. study provides the clearest example of using structural graphs for functional prediction. Proteins can be represented as graphs in which residues or atoms are nodes and spatial contacts are edges. Graph neural networks can then learn local and global structural patterns associated with function.

**Strengths**

- Uses three-dimensional relationships that sequence models may not represent explicitly.
- Can identify structurally conserved functional sites despite sequence divergence.
- Supports residue-level or site-level interpretation in principle.
- May benefit from the rapidly expanding availability of predicted structures.

**Limitations**

- Depends on structure quality and coverage.
- Static structures may not capture conformational dynamics.
- Function may depend on ligands, cofactors, oligomeric state, post-translational modifications, or cellular context.
- Predicted structures can contain uncertain regions that affect downstream classification.
- Structural similarity does not always imply identical biochemical function.

The relevant paper must be checked for whether structures were experimental, predicted, or mixed, and for the treatment of uncertainty.

---

## 3. Network and interaction-based models

Protein–protein interaction prediction and graph learning can provide functional information from biological networks. These methods may infer function from interaction neighborhoods, complexes, or network topology.

**Strengths**

- Incorporate systems-level context.
- Can support annotation for proteins with limited sequence or structural information.
- May capture pathway and complex membership.

**Limitations**

- Interaction databases are incomplete and biased toward well-studied proteins.
- Network edges can be experimentally uncertain or condition-specific.
- Highly connected proteins may appear easier to annotate because of database degree rather than true predictive power.
- Interaction prediction is not equivalent to function prediction.

The supplied records do not establish whether the interaction papers evaluate downstream functional annotation.

---

## 4. Specialized biochemical models

The metalloprotein review illustrates the need for models tailored to particular protein classes or chemical functions. Specialized models may use features such as metal-coordinating residues, sequence motifs, structural geometry, and physicochemical properties.

**Strengths**

- Can incorporate domain-specific biochemical knowledge.
- May perform better than general models for narrowly defined tasks.
- Can target residue-level binding sites as well as whole-protein function.

**Limitations**

- Usually have narrower applicability.
- Positive and negative examples may be scarce or biased.
- Metal-binding annotations may not fully specify biological function.
- Performance may not transfer across metal types, protein families, or cellular conditions.

# Remaining limitations and evidence gaps

1. **Benchmark leakage and redundancy**  
   Closely related proteins may occur in both training and test sets, producing overly optimistic results. Homology-aware or cluster-based splits are needed.

2. **Incomplete functional labels**  
   Lack of an annotation does not necessarily mean lack of function. Treating unannotated proteins as negative examples can introduce substantial label noise.

3. **Annotation bias**  
   Well-studied proteins and model organisms are overrepresented. Methods may perform poorly on proteins from poorly characterized taxa or novel families.

4. **Long-tail and rare-function prediction**  
   Common functions have abundant training examples, whereas rare functions and multifunctional proteins remain underrepresented.

5. **Static and uncertain structural information**  
   Predicted structures provide broad coverage but may not accurately represent flexible regions, complexes, ligand-bound states, or conformational ensembles.

6. **Context dependence**  
   Protein function can depend on cellular localization, expression, cofactors, post-translational modifications, binding partners, environmental conditions, and oligomeric state.

7. **Limited mechanistic interpretability**  
   High predictive accuracy does not establish that a model has learned a causal mechanism. Attribution methods can identify influential residues but require experimental validation.

8. **Generalization to novel proteins**  
   Models may be strong at interpolation within known protein families but weaker for remote-homology proteins or genuinely novel folds and functions.

9. **Evaluation inconsistency**  
   Studies may use different ontologies, label definitions, split strategies, and averaging procedures, making comparisons difficult.

10. **Experimental validation**  
    Computational predictions are most convincing when tested experimentally. The supplied records do not establish how often predictions were validated in the laboratory.

## Bottom line

The evidence supports three main uses of machine learning in protein-function prediction: **sequence representation learning with language models, structure-based graph or geometric learning, and network or interaction-based inference**. These approaches can exploit complementary information, but the supplied records do not yet permit reliable statements about which datasets or metrics were used. Full-text verification is essential, particularly for the handling of homology leakage, incomplete labels, predicted-structure uncertainty, and generalization to proteins outside well-represented families.
