## Overall assessment

The supplied evidence supports a picture in which machine learning is used for protein-function prediction mainly through three routes:

1. **Learning from protein sequence**, including protein language-model representations and transformer-based models.
2. **Learning from structure**, by representing a protein as a residue-contact or geometric graph and applying graph neural networks.
3. **Integrating biological evidence**, such as interactions, ligand binding, metal coordination, omics measurements, or predicted structures.

The strongest directly relevant primary study is Gligorijević et al. (2021), *Structure-based protein function prediction using graph convolutional networks*. The reviews by Bordin et al. (2023) and Yu et al. (2022) provide broader context on protein representation learning and metalloprotein applications. Much of the remaining evidence concerns structure prediction, docking, virtual screening, or disease biomarkers rather than protein-function prediction itself. Consequently, precise claims about datasets, train/test splits, and numerical evaluation results cannot be established from the supplied bibliographic records alone.

## How machine-learning methods are being used

### Sequence-based prediction

Protein sequences can be treated as biological “sentences.” Conventional models use engineered descriptors such as amino-acid composition, \(k\)-mer frequencies, conservation, physicochemical properties, or motifs. More recent systems use embeddings generated by protein language models trained on large collections of unlabeled sequences. These embeddings can then be used for supervised prediction of functional labels, enzyme classes, localization, binding properties, or mutation effects [VERIFY].

Bordin et al. (2023) is particularly relevant to this transition from manually designed sequence features toward self-supervised protein representations. The supplied record does not establish which particular language models, annotation tasks, or benchmark datasets the review emphasizes. Tran et al. (2026), *Rapid directed evolution guided by protein language models and epistatic interactions*, appears to apply language-model information to protein engineering rather than conventional function annotation; its relevance is therefore indirect unless the full text confirms an explicit function-prediction task [VERIFY]. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2025), *Sequence-based virtual screening using transformers*, is primarily about ligand or compound screening, not necessarily general protein-function prediction [VERIFY].

### Structure-based prediction

Gligorijević et al. (2021) uses graph convolutional networks for structure-based protein-function prediction. In this type of approach, residues are represented as nodes and spatial proximity, contacts, or other geometric relationships as edges. The model learns local and long-range structural patterns associated with functional labels. This is important because proteins with weak sequence similarity can sometimes retain similar folds or active-site arrangements.

Predicted structures have expanded the potential input space. AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021) and the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (Váradi et al., 2023) make structural representations available for very large numbers of proteins. However, these resources do not themselves constitute function predictors. They provide structural inputs that may be used by downstream graph, geometric, docking, or annotation models. Whether the cited studies actually train and evaluate function predictors on AlphaFold-derived structures requires verification [VERIFY].

The usefulness of a predicted structure is also likely to vary by region. An inaccurate loop, disordered segment, oligomeric interface, or active-site geometry could affect a downstream functional prediction even when the global fold is plausible. The supplied records support this as a methodological concern, but do not quantify its effect in a particular function-prediction benchmark [VERIFY].

### Specialized and multimodal prediction

The metalloprotein review by Yu, Wang, and Teo (2022) points to a more specialized class of problems in which models may predict metal binding, metal-binding residues, coordination environments, or metal-dependent activity. These tasks can combine sequence, structural, physicochemical, and active-site features. They illustrate why “protein function” is not a single prediction problem: metal dependence, catalytic activity, ligand binding, cellular localization, and biological process may require different labels and evidence types.

Interaction and docking models provide another source of functional information. Gao et al. (2023) examines hierarchical graph learning for protein–protein interaction, while Michalik and Kuder (2024) reviews machine learning in protein–protein docking. Such predictions can help infer pathway membership, interaction-mediated activity, or interface function, but interaction prediction should not be treated as equivalent to complete biochemical or cellular function prediction.

Several other supplied studies use machine learning to identify disease biomarkers, drug-response markers, or therapeutic targets. For example, the LCN2, HBV-related liver failure, lung-cancer efficacy, diabetic retinopathy, and cancer-target studies apply machine learning to biological or clinical prediction. They are not direct evidence for general protein-function prediction and should not be used to infer performance of protein annotation systems.

## Datasets and labels

The evidence indicates several broad dataset types, although the bibliographic information does not identify the exact datasets used in most cases.

### Sequence and annotation databases

Sequence-based systems are generally trained on large protein sequence collections and paired, where available, with functional annotations such as Gene Ontology terms, enzyme classifications, pathway labels, localization categories, or experimentally characterized activities. The specific databases, annotation dates, filtering procedures, and label hierarchies in the cited studies are not given and must be checked in the full texts [VERIFY].

A central difficulty is that functional labels are incomplete and unevenly distributed. Proteins are more likely to be annotated when they belong to well-studied organisms or families, while many sequences have computationally transferred or low-confidence annotations. This can make the apparent learning task partly a prediction of annotation patterns rather than independently verified molecular function [VERIFY].

### Structural datasets

Structure-based models may use experimentally determined structures from the Protein Data Bank, predicted structures, or a mixture of both. The Gligorijević et al. paper should be examined for the source of its structures, the definition of graph nodes and edges, and whether proteins sharing close sequence homology were separated across training and test sets [VERIFY].

The AlphaFold database is relevant because it provides predicted structures for more than 214 million protein sequences according to the title of Váradi et al. (2023). That scale may facilitate structural function prediction, but the database’s structural coverage should not be confused with experimentally validated functional coverage.

### Specialized datasets

Metalloprotein studies may use curated sets of metal-binding proteins or residues, while interaction studies may use experimentally reported interaction networks or structural complexes. The exact positive and negative examples, species composition, balance between protein families, and experimental confidence thresholds are not recoverable from the supplied records [VERIFY].

## Evaluation metrics

The supplied bibliographic evidence does not establish which metrics were reported by the individual protein-function studies. This is a major limitation of the evidence set.

For multilabel annotation tasks such as Gene Ontology prediction, appropriate reporting may include:

- precision, recall, and \(F_1\);
- precision–recall curves and area under the precision–recall curve;
- coverage or recall at a specified precision;
- hierarchical metrics that account for relationships among ontology terms;
- \(F_{\max}\) or semantic-distance measures such as \(S_{\min}\) [VERIFY].

For binary tasks such as metal binding or interaction prediction, AUROC, AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and calibration may be used [VERIFY]. AUPRC is often more informative than AUROC when positive examples are rare, but whether the cited papers adopt this principle must be checked.

For structure-prediction papers such as AlphaFold, metrics such as global structural similarity or residue-level confidence are relevant to structure accuracy, not directly to functional accuracy. Likewise, docking scores or binding-affinity measures should not be interpreted as evidence that a model correctly predicts the protein’s biological function.

A particularly important evaluation issue is the split strategy. Randomly dividing individual proteins can place close homologues, paralogues, or proteins from the same family in both training and test sets. Such a split may produce optimistic results because the model can exploit sequence or structural similarity rather than generalize to genuinely novel proteins. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) provide broader evidence on leakage and reproducibility problems in machine-learning science; application of these concerns to each cited protein study requires full-text verification.

## Remaining limitations

### Incomplete and noisy labels

Protein-function datasets are not uniformly labeled. An unannotated protein is not necessarily a negative example, and computationally inferred annotations may be treated as if they were experimental observations. This creates both label noise and substantial class imbalance [VERIFY].

### Homology leakage and weak tests of generalization

Performance can be overstated when homologous proteins occur in both training and test data. More convincing evaluation would use sequence-identity-controlled splits, family-level or taxonomic splits, and ideally prospective tests on proteins from previously unseen families. Whether the cited direct study uses such safeguards is not shown by the record [VERIFY].

### Distribution shift

Models trained largely on well-studied organisms, common protein families, or familiar functional terms may perform poorly on proteins from underrepresented taxa, divergent sequences, disordered proteins, membrane proteins, or proteins with context-dependent functions. Broad structural coverage does not solve this problem automatically.

### Dependence on structural quality

Structure-based methods may benefit from AlphaFold-scale coverage, but prediction confidence and local structural errors can influence functional inference. Flexible regions, ligand-dependent conformations, multimeric states, post-translational modifications, and environmental conditions may not be adequately represented by a single predicted structure [VERIFY].

### Function is context-dependent

A sequence or structure alone may not determine function. Activity can depend on cofactors, cellular location, expression state, interaction partners, oligomerization, substrate availability, and post-translational modification. Interaction, ligand-binding, and structure predictions therefore provide partial evidence rather than a complete functional explanation.

### Interpretability and experimental validation

An accurate classifier does not necessarily reveal the mechanism underlying its prediction. General machine-learning guidance, including Greener et al. (2021), and the supplied SHAP-related record emphasize the importance of interpreting model outputs, but feature-attribution methods do not by themselves establish causality. Functional predictions ultimately require biochemical, structural, or cellular validation [VERIFY].

### Reproducibility and comparability

Results are difficult to compare when studies use different annotation versions, filtering rules, homologous-sequence thresholds, negative-sample definitions, and evaluation splits. Reproducibility also depends on releasing code, model weights, preprocessing procedures, and exact dataset versions. The bibliographic records do not provide enough information to assess these features for the protein-function studies [VERIFY].

## Conclusion

Machine learning is being used to predict protein function by extracting information from sequence, learned protein-language representations, three-dimensional structure, residue-contact graphs, interaction networks, and specialized biochemical features. The most direct evidence supplied is the structure-based graph-convolutional work of Gligorijević et al., supplemented by reviews of protein language models and metalloprotein prediction. AlphaFold and related structural resources mainly expand the availability of structural inputs rather than directly solving function annotation.

The main unresolved issues are not simply model architecture. They concern the quality and completeness of functional labels, leakage between related proteins, evaluation on genuinely novel families, class imbalance, structural uncertainty, biological context, and experimental validation. Exact datasets and metrics for the cited studies cannot be stated reliably from the supplied metadata; those claims require examination of the full articles [VERIFY].
